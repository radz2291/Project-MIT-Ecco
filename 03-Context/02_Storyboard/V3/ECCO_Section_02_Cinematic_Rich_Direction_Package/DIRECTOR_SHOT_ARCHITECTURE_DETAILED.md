# ECCO ENP3 — SECTION 02: THE FIRST PING

**Film timing:** 0:10–0:22 (12 seconds)  
**Stage:** Step 1 — Cinematic shot architecture, prepared for AI Director / 3D storyboard agent  
**Visuals:** Generated cinematic reference images (one main image per shot), plus a supplementary reference

**Context:** Section 1 ends with the camera beside ECCO, deep underground and almost fully in darkness. Section 3 begins by comparing measured borehole geometry with nominal bit size.

## Main narrative intent

This sequence is not a lecture with one mechanical diagram per explanation. It is a continuous cinematic discovery in three stages:

**Unknown surroundings → first acoustic contact → measured information → reconstructed 360° circumference.**

The film introduces ECCO as something that can measure an invisible world using reflected ultrasonic sound. Do not make it a sequence of disconnected product demonstrations. The audience first feels the discovery, then understands the measurement, then recognizes why repeated measurements matter.

**The single idea Section 2 must communicate:** ECCO sends an ultrasonic pulse, receives its reflected echo, and uses repeated directional measurements as the drill string rotates to reconstruct the surrounding borehole wall.

## SHOT S02-A — FIRST CONTACT

**Film time:** 0:10–0:13 · **Duration:** 3 seconds  
**Primary image:** `S02_A_First_Contact.png`

### Camera composition

An intimate, three-quarter close-up along the ECCO body. The metal extends diagonally through the frame, with a dark rock wall nearby. The composition follows the premium cinematic finishing reference selected for the project: restrained but realistic metallic reflections, tactile dark geology, deep shadows, subtle depth and a narrow, believable sense of downhole space.

The camera is placed *outside* the tool, within an artistic cutaway/annular viewpoint, never literally inside the pipe. Let ECCO's silhouette and a true outward-facing ultrasonic sensor be readable, but allow most of the surroundings to remain hidden. Frame the scene so the small wall-reveal area is visible within the same composition as the originating sensor.

### What happens

We emerge from the darkness of Section 1. ECCO is barely visible.

One pulse fires. A small portion of the rock wall briefly becomes visible. **The echo returns to its originating sensor**, and the illuminated patch fades back toward darkness.

Two more pulses create isolated glimpses of the wall as the tool begins or continues rotating. These glimpses are short, local and clearly separate, rather than lighting the entire borehole.

The pulses are *visual shorthand for inaudible/optically invisible ultrasound*. Light appears only as a cinematic effect to help viewers locate the interaction.

### Camera movement

Almost stationary, with a very subtle forward push. Avoid a dramatic orbit this early; the sound contact, not the camera, should create the tension. The drill string/tool can rotate slowly enough for the audience to read the sensing direction, but the entire assembly turns together.

### Audience experience

*“Something invisible has just been detected.”*

The viewer should feel curiosity and a small reveal, not yet understand the entire imaging system.

### End composition

ECCO remains partially in darkness, but two or three locations on the wall have been briefly revealed. The camera has established a clear visual relationship: **sensor ↔ nearby wall**.

### Timing ideas (director-level beats, not approved frame locks)

- **0:10.0:** Arrival from black, barely legible metal and geology.
- **0:10.7:** One primary pulse and small localized wall flash.
- **0:11.3:** Echo visibly returns; spot quickly dims.
- **0:11.7–0:13.0:** Two abbreviated additional pulse–echo glimpses, setting up the analytic treatment.

### Transition to S02-B

Use the final outgoing/returning echo rhythm as the bridge. Either cut closer to the same visible sensor/rock relation or perform a restrained push that feels continuous. Preserve screen direction of the outgoing pulse and return; avoid spatial disorientation.

### Shot A acceptance

The viewer can locate the emitter and impacted wall, recognize outgoing and returning motion, and feel the darkness without losing visibility of the actual tool.

## SHOT S02-B — SOUND BECOMES MEASUREMENT

**Film time:** 0:13–0:17 · **Duration:** 4 seconds  
**Primary image:** `S02_B_Sound_Becomes_Measurement.png`

### Camera composition

Move closer to the sensor region, keeping both the tool and nearby rock visible. Frame a believable dark negative-space area for a temporary, elegant waveform graphic—preferably integrated with the spatial composition rather than pasted on as a giant software interface.

The sensor remains the origin of each pulse, and wall-hit points remain visible. Material fidelity and lighting should match S02-A. The tighter view can briefly stabilize the camera to give the audience time to read the waveform.

### What happens

We observe another pulse travelling to the wall and returning to the same sensor.

**This time, a delicate waveform emerges within the composition**. It first shows the transmitted pulse, then the returning echo. Highlight the separation in time between transmission and first echo arrival—the basis for estimating sensor-to-wall distance.

Two or three more waveform responses follow, with slightly different arrival times. This conveys that readings vary with different wall distances as the tool changes angular orientation. The waveform treatment gradually resolves into **measured positions on the physical borehole wall**, not a detached data display.

Important: the selected generated image is a *look and sensor/rock composition reference*. It **does not depict the required waveform arrival-time overlay** and shows pulses at different axial levels. The production storyboard must correct that: ECCO's three imaging sensors are around **one sensor plane, 120° apart**, rather than three sensors at different depths.

### Camera movement

Controlled close-up with a subtle lateral move or very slow reframing. The entire tool continues rotating. The waveform appears only long enough for the audience to connect the real pulse–echo interaction with measured travel time; don't freeze the entire film into an engineering chart.

### Audience experience

*“ECCO isn't illuminating the wall. It's measuring how far away it is.”*

This is where the film earns technical credibility without over-explaining.

### End composition

The waveform diminishes. Persistent measurement positions remain associated with wall locations, bridging into the full-circumference shot. These points must be fixed to the wall/measurement representation, **not rotating with ECCO**.

### Timing ideas (director-level beats, not approved frame locks)

- **0:13.0–0:13.8:** Show one clearly understandable outgoing-and-returning event.
- **0:13.8–0:15.0:** Waveform and two-way travel-time/arrival-time graphic appear.
- **0:15.0–0:16.2:** Two or three short additional waveform examples, differing in echo arrival time.
- **0:16.2–0:17.0:** Waveform recedes as measured points persist on the wall.

### Transition to S02-C

The last measurement point should become a visual anchor as the camera broadens its view. Fade off most analytic waveform elements, retaining the spatial data accumulated so far.

### Shot B acceptance

The viewer understands that the same sensor sends and listens. The travel-time relationship is legible, and the waveform leads back to physical wall positions rather than becoming a detached UI demonstration.

## SHOT S02-C — THE HIDDEN CIRCUMFERENCE

**Film time:** 0:17–0:22 · **Duration:** 5 seconds  
**Primary image:** `S02_C_The_Hidden_Circumference.png`

### Camera composition

Start near the same three-quarter view as S02-B, then pull back and rise slightly toward an **oblique along-axis view** of ECCO. The move should expose the annulus and rock wall on more sides without becoming an abrupt top-down technical drawing.

This change of perspective helps viewers understand that ECCO measures in multiple directions **around** the tool, rather than only the wall previously visible in front of the sensor. The geometry remains physically spatial and irregular. A partial, restrained cutaway or selectively transparent near-side rock surface is permitted to show the hidden circumference as a cinematic explanatory device.

### What happens

The **entire drill string rotates**, carrying ECCO and its three ultrasonic sensors around the longitudinal axis. Repeated pulse–echo readings contribute points at different azimuths.

Measurements progressively accumulate at the correct locations on the borehole wall. The points can connect into a light, irregular circumferential trace. The reveal is *progressive*: first separate measurements, then partial arcs, then the first full measured cross-sectional outline. The tool must not project an instantaneous full-volume hologram or perfectly round tunnel.

The end frame is the **first reconstructed circumferential measurement at one sensor plane**. A hint of nearby axial continuity is fine, but the full borehole geometry through depth is reserved for **Section 3**.

**Engineering note:** the actual sensor positions belong on one plane, separated by 120°; the generated reference shows a glamorous end-on sonar-like effect and is not an exact depiction of that sensor arrangement. Do not place the imaging sensor on the tool's axial tip simply because the illustration does so.

### Camera movement

One continuous, deliberate pullback and shallow arc, retaining recognizable ECCO geometry. No abrupt top-down cut. Rotate the tool coherently as one assembly. Prefer changing occlusion and selective wall transparency to forcing a physically impossible camera path through solid rock.

### Audience experience

*“All those tiny measurements are building a complete picture of the unseen borehole.”*

### End composition

ECCO remains visually central inside a dark rock wellbore. An irregular, nearly complete or complete 360° outline is now readable around the tool at the sensor station. This is the handoff into Section 3: actual shape compared with nominal bit-size reference.

### Timing ideas (director-level beats, not approved frame locks)

- **0:17.0–0:18.3:** Camera begins a shallow rise and retreat; data dots survive from S02-B.
- **0:18.3–0:20.1:** More readings resolve around previously unseen azimuths; near-side obstruction fades selectively.
- **0:20.1–0:21.3:** Partial arcs connect into a coherent but uneven circumference.
- **0:21.3–0:22.0:** The first complete ring holds long enough to register before Section 3 introduces a white nominal bit-size reference.

### Transition to Section 3

**Match-cut or continue the oblique camera axis** into the Section 3 first image. Keep the ring in place; add the nominal drill-bit-size reference only in the next shot, allowing the audience to discover measured deviation from nominal size.

### Shot C acceptance

The circumference is perceived as a result of repeated sensing and rotation. The wall representation stays attached to the rock, the tool remains legible, and the final ring can serve as the beginning of the Section 3 geometry reveal.

## Approved engineering facts and boundaries (for all three shots)

1. **Sensor arrangement:** ENP3 has **3 outward-facing ultrasonic pulse–echo imaging sensors, 120° apart in a single axial plane**. The sensor plane is specified as 60 cm above the lower pin shoulder. The mud sensor is separate. Do not confuse round ports or the hydraulic pads with imaging sensors.
2. **Rotary operation:** In the depicted rotating drilling mode, the **whole connected drill string and ECCO body rotate together**. The client specifies clockwise rotation when viewed from surface looking toward well bottom. There is no independently spinning optical/acoustic head inside a stationary body.
3. **Echo physics:** A pulse passes through the drilling fluid to the wall and the reflected signal returns to the sensor. **Same sensor = transmit and receive**. A one-way beam does not communicate pulse–echo measurement.
4. **Distance:** Two-way travel time and corrected mud sound speed estimate the sensor-to-wall stand-off. In idealized terms `distance ≈ velocity × (echo_time − transmit_time) / 2`. Borehole radius also needs the sensor's radial position/eccentricity; a waveform alone does not directly supply absolute radius.
5. **Repeated samples:** More readings around changing azimuths yield a *set of radial wall measurements*. A full rotation contributes coverage, but one revolution is not literally a single instantaneous data 'photo'. Sampling can be dense; the color-coded 3D surface and through-depth reconstruction belong to Section 3.
6. **Open-hole environment:** Bare, irregular geological rock wall; NOT the steel casing, cement or an empty optically clear tunnel. The annular volume contains drilling mud even if visually simplified for legibility.
7. **Camera:** A virtual CGI viewpoint can show through selectively cut-away rock or mud for explanation. This transparency is *not* a literal property of the underground scene.
8. **Color and lighting:** Cyan glow is a stylized way to visualize invisible acoustic energy; the pulse does not create physical blue light underground. No sweeping blue lasers, front-mounted optical spotlights or rotating scan heads.
9. **Product accuracy:** The generated images are **cinematic inspiration**, NOT approved ENP3 CAD or authoritative sensor placement. Production must use the verified client Blender/CAD asset and confirm which actual openings correspond to imaging sensors.
10. **No invented field data:** The shot is a representation of the measurement principle, not actual Mi-VU numerical measurements or a validated performance simulation. Client engineering confirmation remains separate from aesthetic approval.
