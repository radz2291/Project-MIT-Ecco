# Discover Creative Taste

Extract **evidence-backed creative judgment**, not a fixed style recipe. Help a person recognize what they prefer, why it works for them, where it applies, and when a different treatment is preferable. Preserve their agency, ambiguity, and ability to change their mind.

## Scope

- **Own:** taste discovery, carefully chosen reference comparisons, interviews, evidence capture, hypothesis testing, preference revisions, and a portable taste-profile draft.
- **Do not own:** deciding an entire project's final brand identity, constructing media, implementing camera rigs, rendering designs, verifying engineering facts, or converting preferences into mandatory templates. Pass evidence to other direction/execution workflows if requested.
- **Separate:** (1) someone's enduring personal taste, (2) suitability to a particular client/project/audience, and (3) technical or commercial constraints. One does not automatically imply another.
- **Never bake in** the skill author's preferences, a particular studio's style, or a client's existing guidelines. Load user/project sources only when relevant and accessible.

## Inputs and start condition

Start with whatever the person has already said, selected, rejected, or provided. Existing reactions in the current conversation are valid input. If the target person or creative domain is unclear, ask **one** concise question. Do not impose a large preference questionnaire.

Possible inputs: specific works/links/timestamps, artist portfolios, stills, short clips, sound samples, current project constraints, previously selected options, free-form reactions, and rejected variants. If no references are available, elicit one feeling or one example and offer a **small, diverse** reference set or original conceptual comparisons; do not claim to have inspected unseen works.

If asked for online references, verify the work and its authorship before describing particulars. Refer to a work's qualities, not a formula for copying its protected execution.

## Procedure

### 1. Frame the discovery question

Identify what is being tested and why: overall creative voice, composition, color, motion, pacing, wording, texture, interaction, or suitability to a specific project. Narrow to a single useful distinction for each turn.

State the current level of certainty without pretending the person has a fully formed style. Do **not** seek universal consistency as an end in itself.

### 2. Curate contrasts, not merely impressive examples

Present about **3–5 genuinely distinct examples** when opening a new domain; fewer for follow-ups. For each, provide a work to inspect and **one diagnostic observation/question**. Prefer comparably relevant, high-quality references, including alternatives that could challenge an early hypothesis.

Preserve reference granularity: distinguish reaction to a specific shot/work, an artist's larger portfolio, and the person's first glance. Avoid inferring an artist-wide rejection from one example.

When the person simply wants to describe a known work, skip curation and analyze their words.

### 3. Capture feedback as observations before interpretation

Record exact user wording when possible, including expressive phrases like "the picture breathes," "premium but simple," or "the movement tells a story." Record whether it is a like, dislike, tentative interest, or context-specific fit.

Map observations to *candidate* dimensions: subject/spatial placement, hierarchy, narrative wording, rhythm, perceived material quality, framing, depth, emotion, linework, etc. Label those dimensions as an **agent interpretation** unless the person explicitly named them.

Always preserve three distinctions:
1. **Observed:** what the person directly said or selected.
2. **Hypothesis:** the causal reason or general pattern the agent infers.
3. **Undecided:** what the feedback has not established.

Do not equate an aesthetic adjective with a camera value, color code, typeface, or rendering preset.

### 4. Ask the smallest discriminating follow-up

Ask **one high-information question** when the reason for a preference is unclear. Examples: "Was it the framing or how the shot moved?" or "Do you admire this personally, or is it right for this client?"

When a person gives a richly detailed reason, **do not interrogate them again**. Extract the supported signal and move to a new dimension or propose a focused test.

Avoid repeated confirmation cycles, leading questions, and forced rankings. "Both, depending on context," "neither," "not yet sure," and "I changed my mind" are fully valid answers.

### 5. Isolate uncertain traits with controlled comparisons

When a broad selection could be caused by multiple differences, create two close variants from a **shared content baseline**, modifying one meaningful factor where feasible (e.g. composition but not wording, animation timing but not layout, annotation density but not image style).

Differentiate:
- **Exploratory comparison:** several variables change; useful for identifying a territory, **not** for attributing the preference to one cause.
- **Controlled comparison:** a single variable changes; useful for testing one hypothesis.
- **Real-work test:** the treatment is exercised within a complete project/sequence; useful for discovering contextual suitability.

Say which kind of comparison is being used. Never present conceptual images, descriptions, or generated media as scientifically validated, client-approved, or personally endorsed before feedback.

For complex artistic questions, allow "it is the whole orchestration" as a real observation; do not force decomposition beyond what the person can meaningfully judge.

### 6. Form a contextual preference, not a prohibition

For each supported preference, produce the following compact reasoning unit:

- **Preference:** the person's favored quality, stated in ordinary language.
- **Evidence/scope:** exact work/test, direct reaction, and whether it was first-glance, detailed, project-specific, or verified in production.
- **Why it may work:** labeled interpretation, not attributed as a user quote.
- **Best-fit situations:** plausible contexts; confirm if important.
- **Countercase/exception:** where another treatment can be stronger.
- **Status:** confirmed reaction / inferred hypothesis / unresolved; whether an actual artifact was reviewed.

Replace "always use X" with "consider X when it serves Y; compare Z when ...". Strong preferences can still be strong without becoming universal rules.

### 7. Reconcile revisions without erasing history

When someone changes a prior choice, accept the newer feedback **as the active selection for the named context**; retain the former choice as dated/historical evidence. Do not portray revision as inconsistency or try to defend the agent's earlier explanation.

Record relationships: **supersedes**, **qualifies**, **conflicts**, or **context split**. Example: "C now preferred for immersive explanation; A remains good for simple cases" supersedes "A selected" for the former context while retaining A as a valid conditional alternative.

Do not treat missing feedback on an alternative as explicit rejection.

### 8. Consolidate and hand off

After enough evidence, synthesize a **working profile**, not a final immutable identity. Include strong signals, contextual alternatives, open dimensions, contradictory evidence and next useful discriminating test.

Use the [evidence and profile schema](references/evidence-and-profile-schema.md) for storage or agent transfer. Read [worked scenarios](references/discovery-scenarios.md) when you need to calibrate evidence scope, avoid premature inference, or test a revision.

When the user asks to persist a profile, update the **designated** knowledge base only after checking its current structure and ownership. Append/correct evidence first, then edit the interpreted profile. Avoid duplicating a client's brand rules or a project's technical sources in this skill's folder. Don't claim GitHub saves/installed skills unless the tool confirms them.

## Interaction discipline

- Keep turns short: one comparison **or** one diagnostic question at a time unless the person explicitly requests a full analysis.
- Start extracting value from spontaneous language. Quoted adjectives are signals worth investigating, not complete specifications.
- Recognize negative examples as valuable boundaries; ask *which aspect* was disliked before broadening the ban.
- Leave room for novel artistic direction that breaks a settled pattern with a defensible reason.
- Be transparent that generated study images are original illustrations, not replicas of named artists' work.
- Do not claim visual/audio analysis of a link, still, or video unless actually inspected.
- Don't equate taste with objective quality or use fake percentage scores as evidence.
- Reuse existing evidence in subsequent turns; never silently rewrite the person's original words.

## Finish conditions / quick self-check

A discovery slice is useful when the person can see **what they chose, why that choice might matter, what is still untested, and what would be worth comparing next**.

Before handing off, check:
1. Are direct quotations distinct from inferences?
2. Is the chosen work/context precise and is the confidence appropriately scoped?
3. Does the profile allow contextual exceptions and changing one's mind?
4. Would another agent understand this preference **without copying a reference artist**?
5. Is the next question genuinely necessary, or is the current evidence enough?
6. Is any project-specific style/technical authority being accidentally embedded in this reusable method?
