# Spatial Storytelling — Calibration Scenarios

All cases are **synthetic tests**. They exercise directing judgment and handoff behavior, not a particular person’s taste or an approved production plan.

## 1. Meaningful arrival versus generic orbit

**Brief:** A fictional manufactured product has a specific coupling worth revealing. Current plan says "360-degree orbit, very cinematic."

**Expected:** Compose a purposeful beginning, coupling reveal and closing silhouette; choose only the arc segment that reveals the coupling. Ask what changes during travel. Do not prescribe an endless orbit or a universal easing curve.

**Failure:** Recommend slow motion around the whole object solely because fluid orbits feel premium.

## 2. The strongest move is a locked camera

**Brief:** A scientist must demonstrate a precise length comparison; the moving shot makes the relationship hard to see.

**Expected:** Propose a steady, aligned view with unambiguous reference landmarks. Motion is optional; compare with a brief reveal then hold if it aids comprehension.

**Failure:** Treat static camera as automatically boring.

## 3. Camera movement and object movement misattributed

**Brief:** A stationary schematic object is traversed by the camera; narration implies the object itself is advancing.

**Expected:** Distinguish camera motion from physical object movement, and flag the mismatch. Do not animate a fictional operational motion to satisfy style.

**Failure:** Assume apparent image motion establishes product behavior.

## 4. Each stop communicates nothing new

**Brief:** Three camera arrivals show essentially the same face of a sculpture.

**Expected:** Remove or redesign redundant arrivals; each retained view needs new information or emotional effect. Consider one longer still or a cut.

**Failure:** Keep three stops merely because the sequence template expects them.

## 5. Travel has sub-narrative value

**Brief:** A museum installation reveal first appears flat, then as the view moves, hidden depth becomes perceptible.

**Expected:** Plan changing parallax/occlusion so the viewer discovers depth during transit. Define a reliable orientation anchor and a readable arrival. No mandatory path length or arc type.

**Failure:** Interpolate between two arbitrary camera poses and call it storytelling.

## 6. Temporal readability

**Brief:** A close-up reveals a label for a fraction of a second while the camera accelerates and three objects move.

**Expected:** Give the label a readable interval or relocate it to a calmer beat; propose a simple animatic to verify. Do not invent exact milliseconds without output/testing context.

**Failure:** Diagnose only the typography rather than the whole temporal composition.

## 7. Disorienting edit

**Brief:** A chase changes the camera from one side of motion to the other between cuts without orientation cue.

**Expected:** Discuss screen-direction continuity and deliberate axis crossing. Suggest a bridging neutral/on-axis or repositioned view when appropriate; allow a disorienting cut if intentional and supported by the story.

**Failure:** Enforce a rigid rule against any axis change.

## 8. Unknown geometry

**Brief:** The user asks for a buildable camera move from a concept drawing; no 3D scene or dimensions are available.

**Expected:** Produce a **conceptual** path and meaningful frames, label SVG not to scale, and reserve actual world coordinates/lens for scene inspection. Ask about critical geometry only if needed.

**Failure:** Fabricate Blender keyframes, camera clearance and 35 mm focal length.

## 9. Editorial alternative stronger

**Brief:** The camera must travel for ten seconds between an establishing view and detail; the journey reveals nothing.

**Expected:** Recommend a cut or transition and explain why the original travel is dead time; maintain orientation via match/landmark.

**Failure:** Prolong the travel with extra artificial camera motion.

## 10. Real subject plus generative finishing

**Brief:** A 3D animation controls a complicated path; an AI video model finishes it but changes the camera's route.

**Expected:** Treat the generated result as a new animation requiring review. Check arrival frames, transit, continuity and subject fidelity; do not assume first/last frame matches guarantee path fidelity.

**Failure:** Report camera choreography preserved without inspecting intervening frames.

## 11. Multiple viewpoints across disciplines

**Brief:** An architecture flythrough, a live-action documentary detail and a game camera all need to convey "entry → discovery → resolution."

**Expected:** Reuse the perceptual beat method but adapt camera freedom, physical access and interactive navigation to the medium. Do not force a Blender rig, a fixed lens, or identical motion.

**Failure:** Use one static orbit preset as a universal solution.

## 12. Artistic preference conflicts with spatial truth

**Brief:** A client wants a continuous reveal but verified geometry makes a physical path impossible.

**Expected:** Preserve the truth; redesign with cuts, a schematic or a visibly illustrative transition. Identify which authority must verify any technical claim.

**Failure:** Invent new geometry or impossible camera access while presenting the shot as real.

## Acceptance test

For a trial run, use at least four scenarios: an actual purposeful moving shot, a static-or-cut alternative, a technical/geometry restriction and a multi-shot continuity case. Check that the agent:

- Describes **what the image communicates** before choosing the camera path.
- Gives transitions meaningful new perceptual information.
- Makes motion authority explicit.
- Can choose **not to move**.
- Avoids fabricated dimensions, timing and approval.
- Proposes a cheap visual test and observes the limits of the evidence.
- Remains independent of a named artist's or client's aesthetic.

These scenarios are fixtures, **not evidence of completed runtime tests**.
