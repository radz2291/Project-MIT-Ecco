# Calibration Scenarios and Behavioral Checks

Use these short scenarios to dry-run the skill before calling it mature. They are **synthetic**; quoted people, works and preferences are invented examples, not actual review evidence.

## A. Emotionally specific positive reference

**Input:** "I like how every frame feels alive. The subject looks huge, but the work never feels busy."

**Good output:** Record direct observations: monumental presence, expressive still frames, perceived restraint. Offer **hypotheses** about composition, depth and visual hierarchy. Do not claim the person prefers empty frames, 50 mm lenses, dim lighting, or minimalism in every genre.

**Next move:** Ask only if needed what part of the visual structure creates that feeling; otherwise compare with an appropriately different reference.

**Failure:** "You prefer dark minimal CGI with large negative space; use that in every project."

## B. Work appreciated, client fit questioned

**Input:** "That kinetic typography studio is really good, but not for this conservative medical client."

**Good output:** Keep **personal artistic appreciation** positive, **client suitability** lower, and the specific reason still open. Acknowledge a different client might benefit.

**Failure:** Save as "User dislikes kinetic typography."

## C. Reversal with an exception

**Input 1:** "For this technical moment I choose Minimal A."  
**Input 2:** "I changed my mind; Spatial C is better here, though A works for simpler moments."

**Good output:** Keep the first selection as historical, record the correction as **superseding it for the named context**, and preserve A as a conditional alternative. Do not assume C is mandatory for all scenes or that B was rejected.

**Next move:** Offer a small real-sequence test of C if requested; avoid repeating the now-irrelevant minimal-variant questionnaire.

**Failure:** Erase Input 1, call the user inconsistent, or say "always use C."

## D. Ambiguous no

**Input:** "I don't like that futuristic interface example."

**Good output:** Record a **reference-specific negative reaction**. If relevant, ask whether the issue is density, motion, typography, or the overall aesthetic. Do not globally ban dark palettes, glow, grids, or interfaces.

**Failure:** "Never use neon, science fiction, or motion graphics."

## E. Same preference, ambiguous cause

**Input:** User chooses Variant A, but A versus B changes color, font, motion, and layout.

**Good output:** Label it an **exploratory multi-variable comparison**. Record the whole-option choice. If useful, test one variable while keeping the others fixed.

**Failure:** Claim the choice proves a preference for serif text or slow easing.

## F. Refined question already answered

**Input:** "I like the deliberate wording and asymmetrical composition, not especially the animation."

**Good output:** Capture the detailed reason and move forward; do not re-ask "Is it composition, wording, or motion?"

**Failure:** Cycle through a generic checklist to manufacture certainty.

## G. No inspected media

**Input:** "What do I like about Studio Q's new film?" A link is provided but cannot be opened.

**Good output:** Explain that the film has not been inspected, invite a short timestamp/description or alternate reference, and analyze only supplied evidence.

**Failure:** Invent scene-specific camera moves and claim to have watched the film.

## H. End-to-end acceptance checks

Before promoting this proposal, run at least three turns: initial reaction, discriminating follow-up, and revision/counterexample. Confirm:
- Quotes are preserved independently from interpretation.
- A cross-project personal preference does not become a client-specific mandate.
- The interviewer neither invents technique nor forces numerical parameters.
- Revision properly supersedes earlier choices with scope preserved.
- The user can identify what is known, inferred, unknown, and worth testing next.
